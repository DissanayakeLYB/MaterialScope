Pass a current through copper and electrons stream freely; try the same through glass and nothing happens. The difference is not a matter of degree — it is a difference of **electron states**. This lesson develops the band model that explains why some solids conduct, why some insulate, and why a third class — semiconductors — sits precisely in between.

## Ohm's law, microscopically

The macroscopic statement σ = J/E (current density J, applied field E) hides a microscopic one. The current is carried by electrons of charge e and number density n, moving with an average drift velocity v_d:

```
σ = n |e| μ
```

where **μ** is the electron **mobility** (drift velocity per unit field, m²/(V·s)). Conductivity σ (units S/m = (Ω·m)⁻¹) is the product of three things: how many carriers, how much charge each carries, and how easily they move. Metals conduct because n is enormous; semiconductors conduct poorly because n is tiny — until we change it (next lesson).

| Material | Resistivity ρ (Ω·m) | Conductivity σ (S/m) |
| -------- | ------------------- | -------------------- |
| Silver   | 1.6 × 10⁻⁸          | 6.3 × 10⁷            |
| Copper   | 1.7 × 10⁻⁸          | 5.9 × 10⁷            |
| Aluminum | 2.7 × 10⁻⁸          | 3.8 × 10⁷            |
| Iron     | 9.7 × 10⁻⁸          | 1.0 × 10⁷            |
| Nichrome | 1.1 × 10⁻⁶          | 9 × 10⁵              |
| Si (intrinsic) | ~2.3 × 10³     | ~4 × 10⁻⁴            |
| Glass    | ~10¹²               | ~10⁻¹²               |

The table spans **20 orders of magnitude** — the largest property range in all of materials science. No mechanical property comes close.

## From atomic orbitals to energy bands

In an isolated atom, electrons occupy discrete energy levels. Bring billions of atoms together and each level splits into a dense bundle of slightly different energies — a **band**. Two facts follow:

- **Bands are separated by gaps.** Between the occupied bands there are energy ranges no electron state exists in — **band gaps**, E_g.
- **Conduction needs empty states.** An electron can accelerate under an electric field only if nearby *empty* states are available to move into. A completely full band conducts nothing.

This is the whole theory in one sentence: **conductivity is decided by whether electrons can reach empty states.**

<Callout type="note" title="The three classes">
  - **Metals:** a partially filled band (e.g. the 4s band of copper) — empty
    states adjacent, electrons move freely, conductivity ~10⁷ S/m.
  - **Insulators:** a filled valence band separated from the empty conduction
    band by a wide gap (E_g > ~3 eV) — thermal energy cannot promote
    electrons across, so no current.
  - **Semiconductors:** same filled/empty structure, but with a small gap
    (E_g ≈ 0.5–2.5 eV) that thermal energy *can* occasionally bridge.
</Callout>

## Why copper conducts and diamond doesn't

Copper's 4s band is half-filled: electrons have plenty of nearby empty states, so a field nudges them into motion — and the delocalized electron sea from the bonding lesson is exactly this partially filled band. Diamond, by contrast, has a fully filled valence band and a gap of 5.5 eV. At room temperature, essentially no electrons are excited across that gap, so diamond is an insulator — the best thermal conductor known, yet electrically dead. The same covalent network that makes diamond the hardest material also makes it the purest insulator among crystals.

## Temperature: metals get worse, semiconductors get better

In a metal, higher temperature means more vigorous lattice vibrations, and vibrating atoms scatter electrons — **resistivity rises** with temperature (roughly linearly: ρ ≈ ρ₀[1 + α(T − T₀)], with α ≈ 0.004 K⁻¹ for copper). In a semiconductor, higher temperature excites more electrons across the gap — **conductivity rises** steeply. The same knob, two opposite effects, one fingerprint to tell the classes apart on a lab bench.

| Class      | ρ at 20 °C (Ω·m) | Temperature coefficient |
| ---------- | ---------------- | ----------------------- |
| Metal      | 10⁻⁸ – 10⁻⁶      | Positive (resistivity ↑ with T) |
| Semiconductor | 10⁻³ – 10⁵    | Negative (resistivity ↓ with T) |
| Insulator  | 10⁸ – 10¹⁶       | Negative (weak)         |

## Worked example: how many electrons carry the current?

Copper has a resistivity of 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m and an electron mobility of 0.0043 m²/(V·s) (43 cm²/V·s). Estimate the number density of conduction electrons.

<details>
  <summary>Solution</summary>

  ```
  σ = 1/ρ = 1 / 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ = 5.9 × 10⁷ S/m
  n = σ / (|e| μ) = 5.9 × 10⁷ / (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 0.0043)
    = 5.9 × 10⁷ / 6.9 × 10⁻²²
    ≈ 8.6 × 10²⁸ electrons/m³
  ```

  Copper's atomic density is ~8.5 × 10²⁸ atoms/m³ — so roughly **one
  conduction electron per atom**. That is the electron sea made quantitative:
  every copper atom donates one free electron, and there is nothing more to
  tune. Metals are metals because n is fixed at its maximum.

  **Practice:** the same calculation for silicon — intrinsic n_i ≈ 1.5 × 10¹⁶
  m⁻³ and μ ≈ 0.135 m²/(V·s). Compute σ.
</details>

<details>
  <summary>Answer</summary>

  ```
  σ = n |e| μ = 1.5 × 10¹⁶ × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 0.135 ≈ 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ S/m
  ```

  About **10 orders of magnitude below copper** — silicon is a poor conductor
  by itself, which is exactly the problem doping solves next lesson.
</details>
