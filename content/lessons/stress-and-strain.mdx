If you hang a 100 kg weight from a steel cable and from a rubber band, both stretch — but the cable barely moves and the rubber band stretches enormously. Is the rubber band "weaker"? Not necessarily: a thick rubber band can easily hold more force than a thin steel wire. To talk about mechanical behavior we need quantities that do not depend on how *much* material is there. Those quantities are **stress** and **strain**.

<Callout type="note" title="Key idea">
  Stress normalizes force by cross-sectional area; strain normalizes
  deformation by original length. Two specimens of different size but the
  same material, shape, and state of stress deform identically.
</Callout>

## Stress: force per unit area

The **engineering stress** σ is the applied force F divided by the *original* cross-sectional area A₀:

```
σ = F / A₀
```

The SI unit is the pascal (Pa = N/m²); in practice materials scientists use megapascals (1 MPa = 10⁶ Pa) and gigapascals (1 GPa = 10⁹ Pa). A paperclip held in tension by a few newtons of force already experiences tens of MPa; the steel in a bridge deck is designed to carry hundreds of MPa.

Why the *original* area? Because we want to track the force history without re-measuring the specimen — this "engineering" definition is simple and consistent, even though the true area changes as the specimen stretches (we return to this in the plastic-deformation lesson).

A stress that pulls is called **tensile**; one that pushes is **compressive**; one that acts parallel to a face is **shear** (symbol τ). Tension and compression are the easiest to produce and most of this course uses them.

## Strain: deformation per unit length

The **engineering strain** ε is the change in length Δl divided by the original length l₀:

```
ε = Δl / l₀
```

Strain is dimensionless — a fraction — but it is nearly always reported as a percentage. Doubling the length of a specimen means ε = 1.0, or 100% strain. Most engineering metals fail long before 50% strain; elastomers regularly reach several hundred percent.

## The stress–strain curve

Plot stress against strain for a specimen pulled steadily and you get the **stress–strain curve**, the fingerprint of a material's mechanical behavior. Three landmarks matter most:

- **Elastic region** — stress and strain are proportional at first (Hooke's law, σ = Eε); release the load and the specimen springs back exactly.
- **Yield point** — where the curve stops being linear; beyond it the deformation is permanent (plastic).
- **Ultimate tensile strength (UTS)** — the maximum engineering stress the material ever carries; past it the specimen **necks** and soon fractures.

## Worked example: stress and strain in a tension specimen

A steel rod with a 10 mm × 10 mm square cross-section is pulled with a force of 50 kN. A 3.00 m gauge section stretches by 1.5 mm. Compute the engineering stress and strain.

<details>
  <summary>Solution</summary>

  ```
  A₀ = 0.010 m × 0.010 m = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m²
  σ = F / A₀ = 50,000 N / 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m² = 5.0 × 10⁸ Pa = 500 MPa

  ε = Δl / l₀ = 0.0015 m / 3.00 m = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.05%
  ```

  The rod carries **500 MPa** — well below the yield strength of most steels,
  so it deforms elastically — and stretches **0.05%**. Over a 3 m length that
  is 1.5 mm, enough to notice, but far from failure.

  **Practice:** the same load on a rod of 20 mm diameter — compute σ.
</details>

<details>
  <summary>Answer</summary>

  ```
  A₀ = π d² / 4 = π (0.020 m)² / 4 = 3.14 × 10⁻⁴ m²
  σ = 50,000 / 3.14 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.59 × 10⁸ Pa ≈ 159 MPa
  ```

  Four times the area means one quarter of the stress — the same force, a
  much happier rod.
</details>

## Why normalize at all?

Two design questions that stress and strain answer directly:

- **Will it break?** Materials fail at characteristic *stresses*, not forces. A 10 mm bolt and a 50 mm bolt both fail when the stress reaches the material's strength, even though the forces differ by a factor of 25.
- **Will it deform too much?** Structures are judged by *strains*: a bridge that sags 2 cm over a 40 m span has a strain of 0.05% — the same strain a 10 cm gauge section of the same material would show.
