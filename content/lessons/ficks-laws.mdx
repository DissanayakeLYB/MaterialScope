Knowing the diffusion coefficient D tells you how *mobile* atoms are, but not how *much* moves where. That is the job of **Fick's laws**, the two equations that turn "atoms wander" into quantitative engineering: how fast atoms cross a membrane, and how deep they penetrate into a part over time.

## Fick's first law: steady-state flux

When a concentration gradient exists, atoms diffuse down it — from high concentration to low. The **flux** J (amount of material crossing unit area per second, kg/m²·s or atoms/m²·s) is proportional to the gradient:

```
J = −D (dC / dx)
```

The minus sign: flux runs *down* the gradient (from high C to low C). This is Fick's **first law**, valid whenever the concentration profile does not change with time (steady state) — the classic example is a gas diffusing through a thin metal membrane with constant pressures on both sides.

<Callout type="note" title="Reading the units">
  J has units of (amount)/(m²·s); dC/dx has units of (amount)/m⁴; D therefore
  has units of m²/s. D is the same D as in the Arrhenius equation — one
  material property, two uses: first law for how fast, Arrhenius for how
  mobile.
</Callout>

## Fick's second law: non-steady state

Most real processes — carburizing a gear, doping a wafer — are **non-steady state**: the concentration profile changes with time. Fick's **second law** describes that evolution:

```
∂C/∂t = D (∂²C/∂x²)
```

Solving it requires boundary conditions, but one solution is worth memorizing because it appears everywhere in processing. For a semi-infinite solid with a uniform initial concentration C₀, whose surface is held at constant concentration C_s:

```
(C_s − C_x) / (C_s − C₀) = erf( x / (2√(Dt)) )
```

where C_x is the concentration at depth x after time t, and erf is the error function (tabulated; erf(0.906) ≈ 0.80, erf(1.0) ≈ 0.843, erf(1.2) ≈ 0.910). This is the **carburizing equation**.

<Callout type="tip" title="The √(Dt) rule of thumb">
  Diffusion penetration scales as √(Dt) — the depth reached in time t is
  proportional to the square root of time. Doubling the time only extends the
  case depth by √2 ≈ 1.4×. That is why case hardening is done hot and fast
  rather than cool and long.
</Callout>

## Worked example: carburizing a steel gear

A 0.20 wt% C steel gear is carburized at 950 °C in a carbon-rich atmosphere that holds the surface at 1.20 wt% C. At 950 °C, D = 1.1 × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s for carbon in γ-Fe. After 8 hours, how deep has the 0.40 wt% C contour reached?

<details>
  <summary>Solution</summary>

  ```
  t = 8 h = 28,800 s
  √(Dt) = √(1.1 × 10⁻¹¹ × 28,800) = √(3.17 × 10⁻⁷) = 5.63 × 10⁻⁴ m

  (C_s − C_x) / (C_s − C₀) = (1.20 − 0.40) / (1.20 − 0.20) = 0.80
  erf(x / (2√(Dt))) = 0.80  →  x / (2√(Dt)) = 0.906

  x = 2 × 0.906 × 5.63 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.02 × 10⁻³ m ≈ 1.0 mm
  ```

  After **8 hours**, carbon above 0.40 wt% has penetrated about **1.0 mm** —
  a hard, wear-resistant case over a tough, ductile core. This is exactly
  how gears get their two personalities.

  **Practice:** how deep would the same 0.40 wt% contour reach after 2 hours
  instead of 8?
</details>

<details>
  <summary>Answer</summary>

  ```
  √(Dt) = √(1.1 × 10⁻¹¹ × 7200) = 2.81 × 10⁻⁴ m
  x = 2 × 0.906 × 2.81 × 10⁻⁴ ≈ 0.51 mm
  ```

  Half the time gives half the depth (since depth ∝ √t, a factor of 4 in
  time gives a factor of 2 in depth) — about **0.5 mm**.
</details>

## What controls the rate?

Three knobs, in order of leverage:

1. **Temperature** — through D = D₀ exp(−Q/RT), the exponential makes temperature the dominant knob (the previous lesson's 70× example).
2. **Time** — depth grows as √t, so doubling case depth requires 4× the time.
3. **The driving gradient** — a bigger (C_s − C₀) difference raises the flux proportionally in the first law.

There is no "turn the dial faster" without raising temperature, and raising temperature has consequences (grain growth, distortion) — which is why diffusion process design is a balancing act, not just a recipe.
