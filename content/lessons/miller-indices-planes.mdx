A crystal is a repeating pattern of points, and materials scientists constantly need to name specific **planes** and **directions** inside it — the plane a crystal cleaves along, the direction a dislocation slides, the planes that scatter X-rays. Miller indices are that naming system: three integers that identify an entire family of parallel planes (or directions) by their orientation.

## Directions: [uvw]

A crystallographic direction is a vector between two lattice points. To index it: write the vector's components along the `a`, `b`, and `c` axes in units of the lattice parameters, reduce them to the smallest set of integers, and enclose them in square brackets — `[100]`, `[110]`, `[111]`. A negative component gets a bar over the digit (read "bar one, one, zero" for `[1̄10]`). Directions related by the crystal's symmetry form a family written in angle brackets, `⟨uvw⟩`: in a cubic crystal `⟨100⟩` covers all six cube-edge directions.

## Planes: (hkl)

Indexing a plane takes four steps:

1. Find where the plane intercepts the `a`, `b`, and `c` axes, in units of the lattice parameters.
2. Take the reciprocals of those intercepts.
3. Reduce to the smallest set of integers.
4. Enclose in parentheses: `(hkl)`.

A plane parallel to an axis intercepts it at infinity, and the reciprocal of infinity is **zero**. Negative intercepts get a bar: a plane meeting the `b`-axis on its negative side contributes `k̄`. Remember that `(hkl)` describes the whole *family of parallel planes* with that orientation, not a single plane; symmetry-related families are written `{hkl}`.

Some familiar examples: `(100)` are the cube face planes, `(110)` the face-diagonal planes, `(111)` the body-diagonal ("octahedral") planes. In a cubic crystal the plane `(hkl)` is perpendicular to the direction `[hkl]`.

The most densely packed planes are the ones atoms actually slide on. In FCC those are the `{111}` close-packed planes; in BCC, the `{110}` planes.

## Interplanar spacing

The distance between adjacent parallel planes `(hkl)` in a cubic crystal is

```
d(hkl) = a / √(h² + k² + l²)
```

Low-index planes are widely spaced; as the indices grow, the planes crowd closer together.

## Why indexing matters

Miller indices are not just notation — they are how scientists and engineers describe a crystal to one another. When X-rays scatter off a crystal, each family of planes (hkl) produces a diffraction peak at a characteristic angle, so a powder diffraction pattern is essentially a list of (hkl) spacings. When a metal deforms, slip occurs on specific dense planes and directions — FCC metals slip on {111}⟨110⟩ — so the available planes predict how a material will behave. And semiconductor wafers are cut along named planes: most silicon wafers are (100)-oriented, which sets the electrical properties of the surface. The three integers in parentheses are a crystal's coordinate system in miniature.

## Worked example: indexing a plane

A plane intercepts the `x`-axis at `x = 2a`, the `y`-axis at `y = 3a`, and runs parallel to the `z`-axis. Find its Miller indices.

<details>
  <summary>Solution</summary>

  Intercepts: 2, 3, ∞. Reciprocals: 1/2, 1/3, 0. Reduce to integers by multiplying through by 6: **3, 2, 0**. The plane is **(320)**.
</details>

## Worked example: interplanar spacing

Aluminum (FCC) has a lattice parameter a = 0.4049 nm. How far apart are its `(111)` planes?

<details>
  <summary>Solution</summary>

  ```
  d = a / √(1² + 1² + 1²)
    = 0.4049 nm / √3
    = 0.2338 nm ≈ 0.234 nm
  ```

  The `(111)` planes in aluminum are spaced about 0.234 nm apart — roughly 0.8 times the diameter of an aluminum atom (R = 0.143 nm).
</details>
