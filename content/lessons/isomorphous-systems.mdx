The simplest alloy systems are **isomorphous**: the two components are completely soluble in both the liquid and solid states, so at every composition the solid is a single phase. You met the classic example — copper–nickel — in the point-defects lesson: both metals are FCC, similar in radius and electronegativity, so every Cu–Ni alloy is one FCC solid solution. This lesson teaches you to read that diagram quantitatively, including the **lever rule**, which computes exactly how much liquid and solid coexist.

## The isomorphous phase diagram

Below is the germanium–silicon diagram — isomorphous like Cu–Ni, but with a wider melting range that makes the two-phase lens easier to see. The axes are temperature (vertical) and composition (horizontal, in at% Ge). Two curved lines divide the map:

- the **liquidus** — above it, everything is liquid (L);
- the **solidus** — below it, everything is solid (α, one FCC/diamond-cubic solid solution);
- between the two, **liquid and solid coexist**.

## Cooling a liquid: the birth of the first solid

Imagine cooling a 50 at% Ge alloy from the liquid. At the liquidus (~1090 °C) the first solid appears. Two surprises:

1. **The solid has a different composition than the liquid.** At 1090 °C the tie line at that temperature reads the solidus at ~75 at% Ge — the first solid is much richer in germanium than the 50 at% melt it grew from.
2. **The high-melting component solidifies first.** Silicon melts at 1414 °C, germanium at 938 °C; the solid always enriches in the higher-melting component. This is the basis of zone refining and of segregation in castings — the last liquid to freeze is enriched in the low-melting component.

As cooling continues, the compositions of liquid and solid follow the liquidus and solidus, and the *amounts* of each change — which brings us to the tool for computing those amounts.

## The lever rule

At any temperature inside the two-phase lens, draw a horizontal **tie line** across the lens. It crosses the liquidus at composition C_L and the solidus at C_S. For an alloy of overall composition C₀, the weight fractions are:

```
W_L = (C₀ − C_S) / (C_L − C_S)
W_α = (C_L − C₀) / (C_L − C_S)
```

Think of the tie line as a lever with the fulcrum at C₀: the fraction of a phase is the length of the *opposite* arm divided by the total length. The closer C₀ is to the solidus, the more solid there is — an alloy nearly at the solidus composition is nearly all solid.

<Callout type="note" title="Why it's called the lever rule">
  The name is mechanical: the tie line is a lever pivoted at the overall
  composition C₀. The phase on the side where the lever arm is *longer* (the
  side C₀ is farther from) is present in the *larger* amount — the fulcrum is
  near the phase that dominates.
</Callout>

## Worked example: the lever rule at 1200 °C

A Ge–Si alloy with C₀ = 40 at% Ge is held at 1200 °C. From the diagram, the tie line at 1200 °C crosses the liquidus at C_L = 33 at% Ge and the solidus at C_S = 55 at% Ge. Compute the fraction of solid α.

<details>
  <summary>Solution</summary>

  ```
  W_L = (C₀ − C_S) / (C_L − C_S) = (40 − 55) / (33 − 55) = (−15)/(−22) = 0.682
  W_α = 1 − W_L = 0.318
  ```

  The alloy is **68% liquid and 32% solid α**. Sanity check: C₀ = 40 is much
  closer to C_L = 33 than to C_S = 55, and the lever rule gives much more
  liquid — the fulcrum sits near the phase that dominates.

  **Practice:** the same alloy at a temperature where C_L = 20 at% Ge and
  C_S = 45 at% Ge. Compute W_α.
</details>

<details>
  <summary>Answer</summary>

  ```
  W_α = (C_L − C₀) / (C_L − C_S) = (20 − 40) / (20 − 45) = (−20)/(−25) = 0.8
  ```

  **80% solid** — cooling moved the tie line down and right, shrinking the
  liquid and growing the solid, and the solid's composition drifted along the
  solidus toward the overall composition.
</details>

## Why complete solubility is rare

The four Hume–Rothery conditions (radius within ~15%, similar electronegativity, same structure, same valence) must all hold for an isomorphous system. Few pairs satisfy them, which is why most binary diagrams look nothing like the simple lens — they show limited solubility, two solid phases, and flat eutectic lines. That is the subject of the next lesson.
