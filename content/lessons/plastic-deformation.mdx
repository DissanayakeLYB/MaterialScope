Beyond the elastic limit, deformation becomes **plastic**: atoms do not just stretch and spring back — they permanently slide past one another. This is the deformation that lets you bend a paperclip, stamp a car body, and draw copper into wire. It is also the deformation you must *prevent* in a load-bearing structure, because it changes shape permanently. The boundary between the two regimes is the **yield point**.

## Why crystals yield: a recap

The stress–strain curve in the stress-and-strain lesson showed a metal yielding around 276 MPa. A perfect crystal ought to be vastly stronger — theory predicts shear strengths around G/10, tens of GPa for a typical metal. The resolution, as you saw in "Line and Planar Defects", is that real crystals are never perfect: they contain **dislocations**, line defects that glide through the lattice and break bonds only a few atoms at a time. Plastic deformation in metals *is* dislocation motion. Remove the dislocations and you get whiskers that approach the theoretical strength; add obstacles and the metal gets stronger — which is the whole game of metallurgy.

## The yield point

The **yield strength** σ_y is the stress at which plastic deformation begins. In practice:

- Steels often show a sharp **upper and lower yield point** — the load actually drops when the first dislocations break free (Lüders bands propagate across the specimen).
- Aluminum and most nonferrous metals yield gradually, so there is no sharp kink. Engineers then use the **0.2% offset method**: draw a line parallel to the elastic slope, offset by 0.2% strain, and read where it crosses the curve. That crossing is σ_y.

```
σ_y = the stress at which 0.2% permanent strain remains after unloading
```

<Callout type="note" title="Why 0.2%?">
  It is a convention — a permanent strain small enough to be harmless in
  service but large enough to measure reliably. The American Society for
  Testing and Materials standardized it so that "yield strength" means the
  same thing in every lab and every spec sheet.
</Callout>

## Strain hardening

After yielding, the metal does not simply flow at constant stress. As dislocations move, they multiply and tangle — new dislocations are created at Frank–Read sources, and they pile up at obstacles. The result is that *more* stress is needed to keep deformation going: the curve rises. This is **strain hardening** (work hardening). It is why:

- cold-rolled aluminum is harder and stronger than annealed aluminum,
- a paperclip is hard to bend back once it has been bent once (the bend has hardened it),
- wire-drawing and rolling make metals stronger — at the cost of ductility.

Work hardening is a *dislocation* effect, so it changes strength but not Young's modulus: E is a bonding property and stays put.

## Necking and the UTS

At the **ultimate tensile strength**, the specimen starts to **neck** — the cross-section shrinks locally. From that point the engineering stress (computed with the original A₀) *falls*, even though the material is still hardening: the true stress, using the actual shrinking area, keeps rising until fracture. Necking is why the curve has a maximum and why a tensile specimen always breaks at the neck, not somewhere uniform.

```
engineering stress σ = F / A₀     (A₀ fixed)
true stress       σ_t = F / A_i   (A_i = current area, smaller after necking)
```

## Ductility: how much can it stretch?

**Ductility** is the ability to deform plastically before fracture, and it is measured two standard ways from the broken specimen:

- **Percent elongation**: (l_f − l₀)/l₀ × 100, using the gauge length marked before the test.
- **Percent reduction of area**: (A₀ − A_f)/A₀ × 100, at the fracture neck.

Ductility matters twice: it is a *forming* property (can you stamp it, bend it, draw it?) and a *safety* property (a ductile part warns by deforming before it breaks; a brittle one gives no warning at all).

| Quantity            | Symbol | Meaning                                   | Typical value          |
| ------------------- | ------ | ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Yield strength      | σ_y    | Onset of permanent deformation            | 250 MPa (mild steel)   |
| Ultimate tensile    | σ_UTS  | Maximum engineering stress                | 400 MPa (mild steel)   |
| Ductility (%EL)     | —      | Elongation before fracture                | 25–40% (mild steel)    |
| Young's modulus     | E      | Stiffness in the elastic region           | 207 GPa (steel)        |

## Worked example: percent elongation

A tensile specimen has a 50 mm gauge mark. After fracture the two halves fit back together and the gauge length measures 63 mm. Compute the percent elongation.

<details>
  <summary>Solution</summary>

  ```
  %EL = (l_f − l₀) / l₀ × 100
      = (63 − 50) / 50 × 100
      = 26%
  ```

  A **26% elongation** marks this as a ductile steel (mild steels typically
  give 20–40%). A cast iron or a ceramic would fracture at well under 1%.

  **Practice:** the same specimen necked from 10 mm to 6.5 mm diameter.
  Compute the percent reduction of area.
</details>

<details>
  <summary>Answer</summary>

  ```
  A₀ = π(10)²/4 = 78.54 mm²     A_f = π(6.5)²/4 = 33.18 mm²
  %RA = (78.54 − 33.18)/78.54 × 100 ≈ 57.7%
  ```

  Reduction of area is usually larger than elongation because it measures the
  local neck, not the whole gauge length.
</details>

## The three ways to make a metal stronger

Because yield strength is set by how hard it is for dislocations to move, every strengthening mechanism is a way of *obstructing dislocations*:

- **Grain-size strengthening** — smaller grains mean more grain boundaries, which block slip (the Hall–Petch relation from the defects lesson: σ_y = σ₀ + k_y d^(−1/2)).
- **Solid-solution strengthening** — solute atoms (like zinc in brass) strain the lattice and pin dislocations.
- **Precipitation hardening** — fine particles (like the η' precipitates in Al 6061-T6) force dislocations to bow around or cut through them.
- **Work hardening** — the dislocations already present tangle with each other.

Each raises strength and typically lowers ductility — the classic strength–ductility trade-off that all materials selection navigates.
