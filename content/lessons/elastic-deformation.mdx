Stretch a steel spring within its limits and it snaps back. That reversible behavior is **elastic deformation**: atoms are displaced from their equilibrium spacing, but never leave their positions — the bonds store the energy and return it when the load is removed. Elastic deformation obeys the simplest and most useful law in mechanics: Hooke's law.

## Hooke's law and Young's modulus

In the elastic region the stress–strain curve is a straight line through the origin:

```
σ = E ε
```

The constant **E** is **Young's modulus** (also called the elastic modulus), the slope of the curve. It measures *stiffness* — how much stress is needed to produce a given strain — and has units of stress: GPa. It is one of the most structure-insensitive properties of a material: heat treatment, alloying, and cold working barely change E, because stiffness is set by the *strength of the atomic bonds*, not by microstructure.

| Material          | E (GPa) | Note                                |
| ----------------- | ------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Diamond           | ~1000   | Covalent network                    |
| Steel             | 207     | Mostly independent of heat treatment|
| Aluminum          | 69      | About one-third of steel            |
| Glass             | 69      | Ceramic, same stiffness as aluminum |
| Wood (oak, along grain) | 11 | Polymer composite                   |
| Polyethylene      | ~1      | Soft, flexible                      |
| Rubber            | ~0.01   | Nearly incompressible, very soft    |

Notice what the table says about *bonding*, not chemistry: diamond and polyethylene differ in modulus by a factor of 1000, and the gap is exactly the gap between covalent-network and van der Waals bonding you saw in "What Makes a Material a Material". Elastic modulus is a bonding property.

## Poisson's ratio: stretching makes things thinner

When you pull a rod, it not only lengthens — it also narrows. The ratio of the lateral (transverse) strain to the axial strain is **Poisson's ratio** ν:

```
ν = −ε_lateral / ε_axial
```

The minus sign is there because the strains have opposite signs: a tensile axial strain (positive) produces a compressive lateral strain (negative). For almost all materials ν lies between 0 and 0.5. A value of 0.5 means perfectly incompressible — the volume does not change (rubber is close); near zero means the material narrows hardly at all (cork is famously ~0 — push it and it does not bulge sideways, which is why cork is used to seal bottle necks).

## Shear and bulk: the other elastic constants

Tension is not the only way to load a material. For design work you also need the **shear modulus** G (resistance to shape change under shear stress τ = Gγ) and the **bulk modulus** K (resistance to volume change under hydrostatic pressure). For isotropic materials all three are linked by Poisson's ratio:

```
G = E / (2(1 + ν))
K = E / (3(1 − 2ν))
```

The bulk modulus is why the two equations are useful: for ν near 0.5 the denominator of K goes to zero — a material that cannot change volume is infinitely hard to compress, which is exactly rubber's behavior.

## Worked example: how much does the rod stretch?

A steel rod, 2.5 m long and 10 mm in diameter, is loaded in tension with 30 kN. Steel has E = 207 GPa and ν = 0.30. Find the stress, the axial strain, the elongation, and the new diameter.

<details>
  <summary>Solution</summary>

  ```
  A₀ = π d² / 4 = π (0.010 m)² / 4 = 7.854 × 10⁻⁵ m²
  σ = F / A₀ = 30,000 / 7.854 × 10⁻⁵ = 3.82 × 10⁸ Pa = 382 MPa

  ε_axial = σ / E = 382 × 10⁶ / 207 × 10⁹ = 1.85 × 10⁻³
  Δl = ε l₀ = (1.85 × 10⁻³)(2.5 m) = 4.6 mm

  ε_lateral = −ν ε_axial = −0.30 × 1.85 × 10⁻³ = −5.5 × 10⁻⁴
  d_new = d (1 + ε_lateral) = 10 mm × (1 − 5.5 × 10⁻⁴) = 9.9945 mm
  ```

  The rod stretches **4.6 mm** and its diameter shrinks by about **5.5 µm** —
  a contraction too small to see but exactly what Poisson's ratio predicts.
  Note the check on Hooke's law: 382 MPa is below the yield strength of
  ordinary steel (~250–400 MPa depending on grade), so the elastic
  calculation is legitimate.
</details>

## Design implications

Two rules of thumb fall straight out of Hooke's law:

- **Stiffness is geometry + modulus.** A beam's deflection is set by E times a shape factor; you can make a part stiffer by using more material or by choosing a higher-E material, but no heat treatment will raise E for you.
- **Elastic energy is (1/2)Eε².** Springs and shock absorbers store energy by straining elastically; a high-E material stores the same energy with a *smaller* deflection, which is why a steel spring is short and stiff while a rubber band is long and compliant.

The elastic region ends where the bonds give way — the yield point. That is the subject of the next lesson.
