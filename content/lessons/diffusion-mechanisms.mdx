A copper atom in a piece of brass does not stay where it was cast. Over years it wanders through the lattice, hopping from site to site, and that wandering — **diffusion** — is how matter moves through solids. This lesson covers the two ways atoms hop, and the equation that predicts how fast: the Arrhenius equation for the **diffusion coefficient** D.

## The two mechanisms

**Vacancy diffusion** moves *substitutional* atoms — atoms of comparable size sitting on regular lattice sites. An atom can only move if a neighboring site is empty, so it jumps into a **vacancy** (the defect you met in the point-defects lesson), leaving a vacancy behind it. Effectively, atom and vacancy trade places. Because it requires both a vacancy to exist and the atom to break free of its bonds, vacancy diffusion needs substantial thermal energy and is relatively slow.

**Interstitial diffusion** moves *small* atoms — carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen — that sit in the gaps between host atoms. An interstitial atom jumps from one interstice to a neighboring empty one without needing a vacancy. It is much faster than vacancy diffusion and needs less energy. This is why a tiny amount of carbon can diffuse through steel in hours while the iron atoms themselves barely stir.

<Callout type="note" title="Which is faster?">
  Interstitial (e.g. C in α-Fe) beats vacancy (e.g. Fe in α-Fe) by many
  orders of magnitude at the same temperature — smaller atoms, no vacancy
  needed, lower activation energy. The chart below makes the gap visible.
</Callout>

## The Arrhenius equation

Both mechanisms obey the same temperature law. The **diffusion coefficient** D (units m²/s) grows exponentially with temperature:

```
D = D₀ exp(−Q / RT)
```

- **D₀** — the frequency/pre-exponential factor (m²/s), related to how often atoms attempt a jump;
- **Q** — the **activation energy** (J/mol), the energy barrier for a jump (for vacancy diffusion it includes the energy to form a vacancy);
- **R** = 8.314 J/(mol·K) — the gas constant;
- **T** — absolute temperature in kelvin.

Because Q sits in an exponential, temperature dominates everything: a few hundred degrees can multiply D by a factor of a million.

## Worked example: how much does temperature matter?

Compute the diffusion coefficient of carbon in α-iron at 500 °C and at 900 °C. For C in α-Fe, D₀ = 6.2 × 10⁻⁷ m²/s and Q = 80 kJ/mol.

<details>
  <summary>Solution</summary>

  At 500 °C (T = 773 K):

  ```
  Q/RT = 80,000 / (8.314 × 773) = 12.45
  D = 6.2 × 10⁻⁷ exp(−12.45) = 6.2 × 10⁻⁷ × 3.92 × 10⁻⁶
    ≈ 2.4 × 10⁻¹² m²/s
  ```

  At 900 °C (T = 1173 K):

  ```
  Q/RT = 80,000 / (8.314 × 1173) = 8.20
  D = 6.2 × 10⁻⁷ exp(−8.20) = 6.2 × 10⁻⁷ × 2.73 × 10⁻⁴
    ≈ 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s
  ```

  Raising the temperature from 500 °C to 900 °C multiplies D by about
  **70**. This is why heat treatment temperatures are chosen so carefully:
  a 400 °C difference changes diffusion times from weeks to hours.

  **Practice:** compute D for the same system at 700 °C (T = 973 K) and
  compare with the 500 °C value.
</details>

<details>
  <summary>Answer</summary>

  ```
  Q/RT = 80,000 / (8.314 × 973) = 9.89
  D = 6.2 × 10⁻⁷ exp(−9.89) = 6.2 × 10⁻⁷ × 5.06 × 10⁻⁵
    ≈ 3.1 × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s
  ```

  That is **~13 times** the 500 °C value — every ~100 °C roughly triples D
  in this system.
</details>

## Why the exponential?

An atom attempts a jump ~10¹³ times per second, but each jump requires the atom (and for vacancies, its neighbors) to be momentarily at the top of an energy barrier. The Boltzmann factor exp(−Q/RT) is the fraction of attempts that clear the barrier, and it rises steeply with temperature. Random jumps average out to a net migration only when there is a reason to prefer one direction — a concentration gradient — which is where Fick's laws (next lesson) come in.
