Every load-bearing component eventually fails — the design question is *how*. A ductile part stretches, warns you, and breaks with a drawn-out neck. A brittle part shatters with no warning at all, often at stresses far below its nominal strength, because of a small crack. This lesson explains that paradox and introduces the quantity that governs it: **fracture toughness**.

## Ductile vs. brittle fracture

In a tensile test, **ductile fracture** follows necking: the specimen thins to a point or forms a cup-and-cone shape, with a fibrous, dimpled surface. The material has absorbed a large amount of energy (the area under the stress–strain curve) before failing.

**Brittle fracture** is different: the crack runs rapidly across a plane with little plastic deformation, leaving a flat, shiny **cleavage** surface. Cast iron, glass, ceramics, and hardened steels at low temperature fracture this way. The energy absorbed is tiny, and the failure is sudden.

| Feature          | Ductile fracture                 | Brittle fracture              |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Plastic flow     | Extensive, preceded by necking   | Negligible                    |
| Fracture surface | Fibrous, dimpled, cup-and-cone   | Flat, shiny cleavage planes   |
| Warning          | Yes — visible deformation        | None — sudden                 |
| Energy absorbed  | Large                           | Small                         |

## Stress concentration: why cracks matter

The nominal stress in a part can be modest while the stress at the tip of a crack is enormous. For a crack of length a with a tip radius ρ, the stress at the tip is roughly:

```
σ_max = σ₀ (1 + 2√(a/ρ))
```

A sharp crack (tiny ρ) and a long crack (large a) both multiply the local stress. Even a small surface scratch in glass — ρ of a micrometer or less — concentrates stress enough to fracture the whole pane, which is why glass is scored before breaking and why engineers polish sharp corners out of metal parts.

## Fracture toughness: K_IC

Fracture mechanics packages this into one material property, the **fracture toughness** K_IC. A crack of half-length a in a plate under remote stress σ becomes unstable when:

```
K_I = Y σ √(π a)  reaches  K_IC
```

Y is a geometric factor (≈1 for a long center crack in a wide plate). K_IC has units of MPa·m^(1/2) and is a *material property* — measured on standardized notched specimens — just like yield strength. The design equation is then:

```
σ_critical = K_IC / (Y √(π a))
```

Doubling the toughness doubles the allowable stress for the same crack; doubling the crack size reduces the allowable stress by √2. This is why fracture toughness, not strength, often sets the size limit on parts.

## Charpy impact and the ductile-to-brittle transition

Fracture toughness varies with temperature and loading rate. The **Charpy impact test** measures it practically: a notched specimen is struck by a swinging pendulum, and the energy absorbed to break it is read off the machine. Plot that energy against temperature and many steels show a dramatic **ductile-to-brittle transition** — high impact energy at room temperature, a cliff, then low energy (brittle cleavage) below the transition temperature.

- **BCC metals** (steel, iron, tungsten) have a sharp transition; the same steel that bends at 20 °C shatters at −40 °C.
- **FCC metals** (aluminum, copper, austenitic stainless steel) stay ductile down to cryogenic temperatures — no transition.

The transition explains famous failures: Liberty ships in the WWII North Atlantic, welded with steel that became brittle in cold water, cracked and broke in half at dock; the Titanic's hull steel behaved similarly. Design rule: never use a BCC structural steel below its transition temperature without checking the Charpy data.

<Callout type="warning" title="Strength vs. toughness">
  Making a steel stronger (more carbon, more hardening) usually *raises* its
  transition temperature and *lowers* its K_IC. The strongest steel in the
  world is useless if a 3 mm crack brings it down. Selection is a balance:
  yield strength for load, fracture toughness for safety.
</Callout>

## Worked example: what crack size is dangerous?

A pressure-vessel steel has K_IC = 200 MPa·m^(1/2) and is designed to carry σ = 300 MPa. With Y = 1, what is the critical half-crack length?

<details>
  <summary>Solution</summary>

  ```
  K_IC = Y σ √(π a)
  √(π a) = K_IC / (Y σ) = 200 / (1 × 300) = 0.667
  π a = 0.444
  a = 0.444 / π = 0.141 m ≈ 141 mm
  ```

  A half-crack of about **141 mm** would be needed to fail this vessel at
  design stress — far larger than any inspection would allow through, which
  is exactly why pressure vessels are made of tough steel. Compare with glass:
  K_IC ≈ 0.7 MPa·m^(1/2), so the same calculation gives
  a = (1/π)(0.7/300)² ≈ 1.7 µm — a speck of dust is a fatal flaw.

  **Practice:** the same steel at half the design stress, σ = 150 MPa. How
  does the critical crack size change?
</details>

<details>
  <summary>Answer</summary>

  ```
  a = (1/π)(K_IC/(Yσ))² = (1/π)(200/150)² = (1/π)(1.778) = 566 mm
  ```

  Halving the stress *quadruples* the critical crack size (a ∝ σ⁻²). This
  quadratic sensitivity is why design codes are strict about both stress and
  flaw size.
</details>

## Summary: the four lessons so far

- **Stress and strain** give the language — force and deformation normalized by geometry.
- **Elastic deformation** is reversible, governed by Young's modulus, set by bonding.
- **Plastic deformation** is permanent, driven by dislocations, shaped by yield and hardening.
- **Fracture** is governed by flaws and toughness, K_IC — and it is the failure mode that gives no warning.
