More than 90% of all metal produced is steel, and every steel — from a paperclip to a bridge — is an iron–carbon alloy with a fraction of a percent to a few percent carbon. The map of that system, the **iron–carbon (Fe–Fe₃C) phase diagram**, is the most used phase diagram in engineering. This lesson puts together everything from the previous three: a partial solubility system (like Pb–Sn), an invariant reaction (like the eutectic), and the phases that give steels their names.

## The phases of iron–carbon

- **α ferrite** — BCC iron. It dissolves almost no carbon (max ~0.022 wt% at 727 °C) because the interstitial sites are too small. Soft and ductile.
- **γ austenite** — FCC iron. The larger interstitial sites dissolve up to 2.14 wt% carbon at 1147 °C. Strong and ductile; the phase you can heat-treat.
- **Fe₃C cementite** — iron carbide, a compound with exactly 6.7 wt% carbon. Hard, brittle, and nearly pure in properties — the stiffening phase in steel.
- **Pearlite** — not a phase but a microstructure: alternating lamellae of ferrite and cementite formed together by the eutectoid reaction, like the lamellar α + β of the Pb–Sn eutectic.

<Callout type="note" title="Carbon is interstitial">
  Carbon does not substitute for iron — it is small enough (0.077 nm radius)
  to squeeze into the interstices of the iron lattice, as you saw in the
  point-defects lesson. That is why the phase diagram has carbon out to
  6.7 wt% (Fe₃C) rather than a wide solid-solution field.
</Callout>

## The eutectoid reaction

The most important feature for steel is the **eutectoid** — the solid-state cousin of the eutectic. On cooling through 727 °C, solid austenite decomposes into two solids:

```
γ(0.76 wt% C) → α(0.022 wt% C) + Fe₃C(6.7 wt% C)     at 727 °C
```

Like the eutectic, three phases at fixed pressure means F = 0: it happens at a single temperature. The product is **pearlite** — fine alternating lamellae of ferrite and cementite — which is why the region below 727 °C is labeled "α + Fe₃C (pearlite)".

Steels are named by their carbon content relative to the eutectoid:

- **Hypoeutectoid steel** (< 0.76 wt% C): proeutectoid ferrite forms first, then pearlite. A 0.2% C steel is mostly soft ferrite with some pearlite — ductile, easy to form (structural steel).
- **Eutectoid steel** (0.76 wt% C): 100% pearlite.
- **Hypereutectoid steel** (> 0.76 wt% C): proeutectoid cementite plus pearlite — harder and more brittle (tool steels, rails).

## Carbon content sets the properties

Every property change in steel traces back to how much hard cementite is present. More carbon means more Fe₃C, so:

| Steel (wt% C)  | Microstructure              | Yield strength (approx.) | Use                        |
| -------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------ | -------------------------- |
| 0.05–0.15      | Mostly ferrite + pearlite   | 200–300 MPa              | Sheet, wire, structures    |
| 0.2–0.4        | Ferrite + pearlite          | 250–400 MPa              | Shafts, gears, beams       |
| 0.76 (eutectoid) | 100% pearlite             | ~500–700 MPa             | Rails, springs             |
| 0.8–1.2        | Pearlite + cementite        | 600–900 MPa              | Tools, cutters             |

The trade-off is the theme of this whole course: carbon raises strength and hardness but lowers ductility and toughness — and, as the fracture lesson warned, raises the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature.

## Worked example: is it ferrite, austenite, or pearlite?

A 0.4 wt% C steel is held at 900 °C, then cooled slowly to 600 °C. Identify the phases at each temperature.

<details>
  <summary>Solution</summary>

  - **At 900 °C**: the A3 line at 900 °C sits at ~0.72 wt% C, and 0.4 < 0.72,
    so the steel is in the **α + γ** two-phase region (or fully austenitic
    just above; either way, no liquid — 900 °C is far below the solidus).
    Actually, 0.4 wt% C at 900 °C: the alloy is **austenite** (the α + γ
    field for 0.4 wt% C spans roughly 740–800 °C; by 900 °C it is all γ).
  - **Cooling through 727 °C**: austenite reaches the eutectoid line and the
    remaining γ transforms to pearlite. Because 0.4 < 0.76 (hypoeutectoid),
    proeutectoid ferrite formed first during cooling, and the final room-
    temperature structure is **proeutectoid ferrite + pearlite**.
  - **At 600 °C**: the steel is **α + Fe₃C** — ferrite grains with pearlite
    colonies — the classic structure of a medium-carbon steel.
</details>

## From diagram to heat treatment

The phase diagram explains the simplest heat treatment: **annealing** (slow cool → coarse, soft pearlite + ferrite), **normalizing** (air cool → finer pearlite), and the hint of what comes next — **quenching** (fast cool). Quench a 0.8 wt% C steel and the austenite cannot transform by diffusion; it becomes **martensite**, a hard, supersaturated BCC-tetragonal phase the equilibrium diagram never shows. The diagram tells you the destination; kinetics tells you whether you get there. That is the difference between equilibrium (this course) and processing (a future one).
