Most binary systems do not mix completely. The lead–tin system — the basis of solder — is the classic example: lead and tin dissolve in each other only partially, so the solid can exist as two distinct phases, and the diagram shows the **eutectic** reaction that makes soldering possible at all.

## The lead–tin diagram

At the far left of the diagram is **α** — lead with some tin dissolved in it (FCC, up to 19 wt% Sn at 183 °C). At the far right is **β** — tin with some lead dissolved in it (up to ~2.5 wt% Pb). Between them, below 183 °C, the two phases coexist. The key feature is the **eutectic point**: at exactly 61.9 wt% Sn and 183 °C, liquid, α, and β all coexist.

## The eutectic reaction

Cool a liquid of exactly 61.9 wt% Sn. It stays liquid all the way down to 183 °C — below the freezing point of *either* pure metal (Pb melts at 327 °C, Sn at 232 °C). At 183 °C the liquid transforms isothermally into two solids at once:

```
L(61.9) → α(19) + β(97.5)        at 183 °C
```

This is the **eutectic reaction**: one liquid, on cooling, becomes two solids. Three phases coexisting at fixed pressure means F = 0 — an invariant point, which is why the transformation happens at a single temperature and why the diagram shows a flat line. The product is a fine **lamellar** mixture of α and β plates that form by simultaneous growth — this structure is called the **eutectic microstructure**, and it is why solders freeze at a sharp temperature instead of over a mushy range.

## Hypoeutectic and hypereutectic alloys

Most solders are not exactly 61.9 wt% Sn. An alloy with **less** tin than the eutectic (to the left, e.g. 40 wt% Sn) is **hypoeutectic**: cooling it, proeutectic α forms first as primary dendrites, and the remaining liquid — now at the eutectic composition — freezes at 183 °C into the fine α + β mixture. The final structure is *primary α + eutectic*. An alloy with more tin (to the right) is **hypereutectic**: primary β plus eutectic. The eutectic itself (at 61.9) is **eutectic composition**: 100% fine lamellar structure, no primary phase.

| Composition        | Structure at room temperature                          |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| 40 wt% Sn (hypo)   | Primary α + eutectic (α + β lamellae)                  |
| 61.9 wt% Sn (eutectic) | 100% eutectic (fine α + β lamellae)                |
| 80 wt% Sn (hyper)  | Primary β + eutectic (α + β lamellae)                  |

## Worked example: lever rule on the Pb–Sn diagram

A 40 wt% Sn alloy is held at 200 °C. From the tie line at 200 °C, α contains C_α = 18 wt% Sn and β contains C_β = 98 wt% Sn. Compute the weight fractions of α and β.

<details>
  <summary>Solution</summary>

  ```
  W_α = (C_β − C₀) / (C_β − C_α) = (98 − 40) / (98 − 18) = 58 / 80 = 0.725
  W_β = 1 − W_α = 0.275
  ```

  At 200 °C the alloy is **72.5% α and 27.5% β**. Note the lever rule flips
  arms compared with the isomorphous lesson: here the phases are α and β, so
  the rule is written with C_α and C_β, with the fulcrum still at C₀.

  **Practice:** the same alloy at 183 °C (below the eutectic, tie line C_α = 19,
  C_β = 97.5). Compute W_β.
</details>

<details>
  <summary>Answer</summary>

  ```
  W_β = (C₀ − C_α) / (C_β − C_α) = (40 − 19) / (97.5 − 19) = 21 / 78.5 ≈ 0.268
  ```

  Essentially unchanged from 200 °C (27.5% → 26.8%): between the solidus and
  room temperature, the alloy is α + β with a slowly drifting β fraction —
  the solvus lines, not the eutectic, control the low-temperature behavior.
</details>

## Why the eutectic matters

Three practical consequences make the eutectic the most important feature in alloy design:

- **Lowest melting point**: the eutectic temperature is the lowest freezing point on the diagram, which is why solders are eutectic or near-eutectic alloys — they melt at a temperature safe for the components being joined.
- **Sharp freezing**: a eutectic freezes at one temperature, giving clean castings with no mushy zone; off-eutectic alloys freeze over a range and can segregate.
- **Fine microstructure**: the simultaneous growth of α and β produces a fine, strong two-phase structure — the basis of cast iron's versatility (the Fe–C eutectic, next lesson).
